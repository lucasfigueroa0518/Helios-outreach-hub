# First-contact outreach

## Output contract

These requirements bind every email this skill produces. When anything later in this document conflicts with them, the contract wins.

1. **Identity and offer up front.** By the end of the second sentence, the reader knows who is writing, that the firm is Helios Group, and the one specific service on offer. One exception: a single opening sentence about this specific reader — their project, their filing, their hire, something they wrote; person or company level only — may come first, moving identity and offer no later than the third sentence. Nothing else precedes identity: no industry observations, no segment generalizations, no warm-up.
2. **One service, named as a deliverable.** Exactly one service per email, selected from the Helios positioning file and translated into the reader's vocabulary at the level of the concrete thing Helios would build or run for them. A category ("AI systems") fails. A menu of two or more capabilities fails. Pick the service that is most likely to convert for any specific prospect.
3. **Length.** Body of 50 to 90 words. 120 is the absolute ceiling, never the target. Two or three paragraphs, none over three sentences. Subject line and sign-off short and plain.
4. **One ask.** A call, a meal, or a reply, in whatever form the reader's world prefers. Concrete enough to answer in a sentence, phrased so declining costs nothing, with nothing attached to it.
5. **Instant fails.** Any of these voids the draft: an opening that generalizes about the reader's segment ("most firms like yours," "X firms tend to"); guessing the reader's internal state ("I'd guess," "I imagine," "chances are"); a category or menu where a named service belongs; a first sentence that stacks clauses; any fact about the reader or about Helios not present in the inputs; an em dash; the first sentence continuing on the same line as the greeting ("Blane, your work…").
6. **Greeting line.** The body opens with the recipient's first name and a comma on its own line, then a blank line, then the first sentence. Never "FirstName, rest of sentence" on one line.
7. **Structural, never lexical.** This system sends across thousands of campaigns and every industry. The requirements above define what must be present, never how it is worded. No opener, sentence pattern, or phrase may harden into a formula; wording varies naturally with each reader and register. Two emails may share structure; they may never share sentences.

## Inputs

The skill runs alongside two inputs and invents nothing beyond them.

**The Helios positioning file.** The sole source for everything the email states or implies about Helios: who the firm is, its services, industries, buyer priorities, differentiators, and outcomes. Select the one service from it that best fits this reader. Never derive organizational facts from anywhere else (not from the sender's own location, title, or territory, and not from memory). If a Helios fact would strengthen the email but is not in the file, omit it. If the positioning file is missing, stop and request it rather than draft Helios facts from nothing.

**The lead data.** The source for facts about the reader. Research can extend it; invention cannot. A fabricated "I noticed" is worse than no personalization.

## Procedure

Work in order. Depth for step 2 lives in the doctrine below.

1. **Intake.** Read the positioning file and the lead data.
2. **The four readings.** The reader's world: its pressures, norms, and register, and what uninvited mail they already get, because this email is sorted against it. The status geometry: the honest posture between these two people; when uncertain, write one step below peer. The structural relation: why a working relationship plausibly makes sense; where real tension exists, name it early. The reason and its resolution: why this reader, now; climb to the highest resolution the inputs honestly support, and weight-test whatever detail you center.
3. **Select the service.** One, from the positioning file, chosen for this reader, named at deliverable level in their vocabulary.
4. **Draft.** Write to the contract, in the register the readings found. The identity sentence is where fluency shows: what it chooses to name proves world-knowledge better than any observation paragraph could.
5. **Strip.** Four essentials survive: identity, offer, reason, ask. Everything that claims, pressures, pads, implies knowledge the sender does not hold, or gives a detail more weight than the reader's world would comes out.
6. **Verify.** Run the checklist. On any failure, rewrite the failing element and verify again. Never patch around a failure.

## Verification

Binary checks. All must pass before the email leaves the skill.

Contract:
- Helios Group and the named service appear by sentence two (sentence three only behind a person-level opener)?
- The service is one, from the positioning file, deliverable-level, in the reader's vocabulary?
- Body 50 to 90 words, hard stop 120; two or three paragraphs, none over three sentences?
- Subject plain and specific, the kind a colleague would write?
- One ask, answerable in a sentence, free to decline, nothing attached?
- Nothing stated about the reader or Helios that the inputs do not contain?
- Greeting is the first name and a comma on its own line, then a blank line?

Sociology:
- Would a thoughtful, busy human in the sender's position have sent exactly this?
- Does anything pretend to know the reader better than the sender does (a guess about their internals, an observation about their segment dressed as sight)?
- Does any sentence match a move in the vendor pattern below? If the reader could name the move, it fails.
- Any prose tell: em dash, rule-of-three cadence, balanced antithesis, hollow intensifiers, uniform rhythm, a summarizing close, greeting-card filler?
- Any overloaded sentence: clause stacks, appositive pile-ups, an introduction chaining identity plus offer plus credential?
- Is formality taken from the reader's world and voice from the sender, tuned to these two specific people?
- Could any sentence be pasted unchanged into a different reader's email? If yes, it is a formula; rewrite it.

## Doctrine

Everything below is subordinate to the contract, procedure, and verification. It governs judgment wherever they do not specify.

### The gate

A senior professional triages unknown mail in seconds, and the triage is a sort: person or campaign, insider or outsider, peer or vendor. The sort runs on signals rather than claims: register, formatting, what the sender takes for granted, the shape of the ask are all read before the content is. Mail sorted as campaign or vendor is dead no matter what it offers. Two consequences. First, the work is mostly analysis: fluency cannot be faked with insider-sounding phrases, only possessed. Second, one detected technique reprices the whole email; a reader who can name the move stops believing every sentence around it.

### The readings, in depth

**The reader's world.** What their week actually contains. The pressures and constraints that shape their decisions: regulatory, structural, seasonal, political. Who else writes to them uninvited and what that mail looks like. The contact norms of their world: some industries do business over meals, some over short calls, some only through introductions. The register their world speaks. Tight precision here is what makes an email land as insider rather than generic; fluency is displayed through effortless understanding, never through a barrage of attempts at connection.

**The status geometry.** The real relation between the two people: peer to peer, junior to senior, unknown to established, principals of adjacent worlds. Each geometry has an honest posture. A junior writer does not fake peerhood: deference performed correctly, a small specific ask, and curiosity about the senior person's experience form a recognized, legitimate approach, often carried by shared tribe. Peers do not perform deference: they are direct and name the mutual ground. The one role that fails at every rung is vendor, the person whose interest in the reader is the reader's budget.

**The structural relation.** How the two worlds relate: competing, complementary, or adjacent. The contact always has a business motive, and this reading locates why a working relationship could plausibly make sense; the email never dresses commercial outreach as social curiosity. The strongest reasons usually live in this structure: a constraint on the reader's side creating needs they cannot serve themselves, a boundary both sides share, a third party both touch. Where tension is plausible, name it plainly and early. Naming the elephant is an insider move; pretending it is not in the room is an outsider one.

**The reason and its resolution.** Why this reader, why now, from this sender. A real reason exists at some resolution: the person (a shared tie, a live event, something they wrote), the company (a project, a filing, a transition), the role or segment, the moment, or the structure between the two worlds. Climb to the highest resolution the inputs honestly support; person-level ground often sits unnoticed in the material provided. A detail earns the center of the email only if the reader themselves would find it significant; the test is "that is a sensible reason for someone to contact me now," not "why is this stranger making so much of that?" A minor specific can season an email; it cannot anchor one. Write the whole email at the resolution where you land. A segment email that knows exactly what it does not know beats a person-level email built on faked intimacy, and openly not knowing the reader's specifics while knowing their world cold is itself a belonging signal. Abort only at true zero (no fluency, no structure, no timing, nothing honest to stand on) and say so rather than write filler.

### The signal economy

Claims are cheap. "Leading platform," "vetted network," "trusted by hundreds of clients" cost nothing to type, so they carry no information except that the sender talks like a marketer. Demonstrated fluency is costly: the specific constraint governing the reader's decisions, their world's shorthand used correctly, the objection anticipated. Convert every claim of quality into a demonstration or cut it. The named service is not a claim; it is orientation. Stated plainly ("we build X for Y"), it informs. Dressed in quality adjectives, it becomes a claim and dies with the rest.

Restraint signals status. People with options do not push. The email reads as if the sender's week is full either way; the less it needs the reply, the more likely it earns one. The reader stays in full control of whether anything happens next.

What earns the ask is the quality of attention the email demonstrates: a real reading of the reader's situation and an honest reason for writing. Promised value is not attention; offering insights, perspectives, or materials as the price of a meeting converts the email into a pitch and the sender into a vendor. The posture is confident and humble at once: confident that a conversation between these two people is worth having, humble in claiming nothing beyond the reason itself.

### The vendor pattern

These moves flip the sort to campaign. They are descriptions of what fires the reader's pattern matcher, not rules to invert.

Fake intimacy in the opening ("Hope you're having a great morning!"). Self-praise epithets ("a leading..."). The fake observation ("I've noticed many companies in your industry..."), which claims sight while confessing blindness. The self-flattering close ("let's compare notes," "happy to share our perspective," "walk you through what we're seeing") and its peer-benchmarking variant ("talk through what other finance teams are prioritizing," "trade perspectives on how other finance leaders are handling this," "compare where we have helped similar operators"): both price the sender's own opinions as the reward for meeting, and both claim cross-client insight the individual writing rarely holds. The email must never lend the writer knowledge that is not theirs to offer. Asking readers to qualify themselves ("Is this something you handle?") or to educate the sender about their own operation. Manufactured exclusivity or urgency. The presumptive close ("let me know what time works best"). The referral fallback ("if not, who should I speak with?"). Announcing brevity instead of being brief. Guilt loops ("if you could let me know either way"). Marketing formatting: bullets, bolding, numbered value propositions, links as proof, cold calendar links, testimonial counts. Long signatures with sales titles, and any unsubscribe apparatus, which declare in writing that this is a campaign.

### Writing it

Form is a signal before a sentence is parsed: short plain paragraphs in one consistent register, one idea, one reason, one ask.

The subject line is the outermost gate. Make it plain and specific, the kind a colleague would write: the shared context, the introducer's name, the actual topic. Benefit language, title-case sheen, and "Quick question" file the email as campaign before it opens.

Geometry sets the posture; the contract sets the structure. A junior writer's frame is deference performed correctly and a small specific ask. Peers are direct; when the mutual ground is person-level it may open the email, otherwise it follows the identity sentence. Peerhood is granted by the reader, never claimed by the sender; when the geometry is uncertain, write from one honest step below, because modesty from a true peer costs little while presumed equality from anyone else fails the gate.

Own the motive: the sender is writing because a working relationship might make sense, the reader knows it, and the email never pretends otherwise, so nothing like "no ask here, just wanted to introduce myself" survives. Close without summarizing, restating value, or pushing. Never close on "if you'd be willing to exchange notes" or its swapping and trading variants; it is not a thing a person says. Keep the signature minimal: name, role if it orients, nothing that sells.

Deliver one email, well read, rather than variants. Offer alternatives only when the geometry genuinely forks and the user must choose a posture.

### The prose

The writing carries as much signal as the content, and none of it can read as machine text.

The em dash pause is the loudest single tell; do not use it. Alongside it: rule-of-three cadence, balanced antithesis ("this isn't X, it's Y"), hollow intensifiers ("truly," "incredibly," "seamlessly"), uniform sentence rhythm, the tidy summarizing close, and greeting-card filler ("I hope this finds you well"). None of it survives.

One word-level ban: when the email refers to the sender's own team or employees, do not call them "people." In this register it reads as filler; write professionals, colleagues, the team, or the specific role.

More important than any single tell is calibration. Tune vocabulary, formality, warmth, and sentence rhythm to these two specific people. A founder writing an investor and a services principal writing an audit partner should not share a sentence between them: one world runs compressed and casual, tolerates fragments, and reads polish as inauthentic; the other runs measured and hedged and reads slang as unserious. The target register is the overlap of the reader's world and the sender's true voice. Where the two conflict, take formality from the reader's world and voice from the sender.

Write with human irregularity: sentences of unequal length, plain verbs, concrete nouns, contractions where the register allows, one thought permitted an extra sentence because it matters. Long sentences are acceptable; overloaded ones are a hard reject. A sentence that stacks clauses until it takes a second read to understand gets split: no program name plus two or three project details plus a qualifying clause in one breath, no appositive stacks after a comma. Self-introductions follow the same rule: one clean sentence for who you are and where you work, not a clause chain, and the identity-and-offer sentence obeys it absolutely: identity and offer in one plain breath, never identity plus offer plus credential plus geography.

Also strip humility theater and self-sabotage: never write "I don't know…", "I won't guess…", or "we don't have a track record in…". If a detail is unknown or unsupported, omit it. Naming a real conflict of interest is not the same as inventing an industry-gap apology.

The finished email must pass two tests. A thoughtful, busy human in the sender's position could have sent it. And nothing in it pretends to know the reader better than the sender does.

### Judging existing drafts

When asked to judge or repair an existing draft, run the readings and the verification checklist on it, and name the signals it is sending before touching a word.
